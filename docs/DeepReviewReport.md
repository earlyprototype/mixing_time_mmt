# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY
---
## Summary
This M.Phil. thesis investigates a novel signal-based method for predicting the perceptual mixing time ($t_{mp}$)—the transition point between early reflections and the diffuse late reverberation tail—in Room Impulse Responses (RIRs) for Hybrid Artificial Reverberation (HAR). The author models eight shoebox-shaped rooms using Allen and Berkley's Image-Source Method (ISM), computes time-varying Fractal Dimension (FD) profiles using a 50-sample sliding-window Higuchi algorithm smoothed with a 200-sample moving average, and evaluates four statistical threshold criteria ($\mu_{\text{late}} - k\sigma_{\text{late}}$ for $k \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ computed over the final 10% of the profile) against empirical perceptual mixing times ($t_{mp50\%}$), finding that Criterion III ($\mu_{\text{late}} - 2\sigma_{\text{late}}$) achieves an explained variance of $R^2 = 93.49\%$.

## Strengths
* The thesis addresses a practical optimization challenge in virtual acoustics and Hybrid Artificial Reverberation (HAR), where accurately identifying the perceptual mixing time minimizes the computational cost of FIR convolution without introducing premature algorithmic artifacts from filterbanks.
* Applying a short sliding-window Higuchi fractal dimension estimator (~1 ms / 50 samples) to track the space-filling geometric complexity of an RIR waveform and calibrating a statistical tail-threshold criterion directly against psychoacoustic perceptual mixing times ($t_{mp50\%}$) is an original and computationally straightforward formulation that avoids Gaussian amplitude distribution assumptions.
* Within the evaluated eight-room simulation setup, the proposed Criterion III threshold ($\mu_{\text{late}} - 2\sigma_{\text{late}}$) demonstrates a strong linear correlation ($R^2 = 93.49\%$) with empirical perceptual mixing time data, providing an encouraging proof of concept for complexity-based RIR analysis.

## Weaknesses
* The direct comparison of $R^2$ values in Table 6.3 introduces a methodological discrepancy in both signal characteristics and sample size: the proposed Higuchi FD predictor is evaluated on $N = 8$ noise-free, unipolar ISM-simulated RIRs (excluding Room 9), whereas the benchmark literature baselines—such as the mean free path model ($V/S$) and Normalized Echo Density (NED)—were evaluated across all $N = 9$ rooms using real-world measured Binaural Room Impulse Responses (BRIRs) containing bipolar sound pressure oscillations, ambient noise floors, and diffuse scattering; the author might consider evaluating the FD predictor directly on the measured BRIRs or re-evaluating the baselines on the identical 8-room simulated subset (Reference Paper: Perceptual Evaluation of Physical Predictors of the Mixing Time in Binaural Room Impulse Responses, Alexander Lindau, Linda Kosanke, and Stefan Weinzierl, 2010; Reference Paper: A Simple, Robust Measure of Reverberation Echo Density, Jonathan S. Abel and Patty Huang, 2006).
* While contemporary room acoustics research explored sliding-window fractal dimension "fractograms" for qualitative RIR texture inspection and the proposed $\mu_{\text{late}} - k\sigma_{\text{late}}$ threshold adapts the late-tail standard deviation thresholding concept from Normalized Echo Density ($1 - \sigma_{\text{late}}$), the proposed first-crossing detection relies on an undocumented hardcoded 1000-sample (~22.7 ms) starting offset (`FDthresh.m`), a 200-sample moving average (`HMW.m`), and post-hoc selection among four criteria on only $N = 8$ points without cross-validation or sensitivity analysis, exhibiting a cliff-edge collapse from $R^2 = 93.49\%$ (Criterion III) to $R^2 = 0.14\%$ (Criterion IV) (Reference Paper: Qualitative Analysis of Texture of Room Impulse Response Using Fractal Dimension, Milorad Paskaš, Ana Gavrovska, Miomir Mijić, and Branimir Reljin, 2010; Reference Paper: A Simple, Robust Measure of Reverberation Echo Density, Jonathan S. Abel and Patty Huang, 2006).
* The MATLAB implementation and quantitative analysis contain several mathematical and physical scaling issues that the author might consider resolving, including an implicit speed of sound of $c = 1000\text{ m/s}$ in the metric ISM delay conversion (`sroomguicbs.m`) that compresses reflection arrival times by ~2.9$\times$ (largely explaining the ~0.32 regression slope in Equation 6.2), asymptotic FD values in Table 6.1a exceeding the theoretical planar curve upper bound of $2.0$ due to finite-sample estimation artifacts over 51-sample impulsive windows, and a unit-conversion error in Section 6.2 claiming that the 325-sample intercept at $44.1\text{ kHz}$ represents less than $1\text{ ms}$ rather than $7.37\text{ ms}$.

2. **Inconsistencies Across All-Pass and Comb Filter Equations, Block Diagrams, and Pole-Zero Plots (Section 2.4.2, PDF p. 24; Appendix 9.5, PDF pp. 96–99)**

     $$Y(z) = -g X(z) + z^{-m} W(z) = \left(-g + \frac{z^{-m}}{1 - g z^{-m}}\right) X(z) = \frac{-g + (1 + g^2)z^{-m}}{1 - g z^{-m}} X(z)$$

This indicates that the plot was generated using a non-reciprocal numerator polynomial (such as $z^M + g$ or $1 + g z^{-M}$) rather than the all-pass numerator $-g + z^{-M}$.
   * **Comb Filter Delay Variable Mismatch (Appendix 9.5.1, PDF p. 96):** On PDF p. 96 (Thesis p. 85), the comb filter difference equation, transfer function, and expanded impulse response use $M$ to denote the delay length in samples, but the closed-form summation on the second line of the impulse response switches the delay variable from $M$ to an undefined symbol $D$:

   * **Incorrect Figure References and Captions in Appendix 9.5:** On PDF p. 98 (Thesis p. 87), the text states: *"The block diagram of an all-pass filter can be seen in Figure 9.5.1a, and it's impulse response in Figure 9.5.2b."* Figure 9.5.1a is actually the comb filter; both the block diagram and impulse response of the all-pass filter are in Figure 9.5.2a, while Figure 9.5.2b shows its frequency response. 3. **Convolution Equations, Transform-Domain Notation, and Block Overlap Counts (Section 2.3.1, PDF pp. 20–21; Appendix 9.2, PDF pp. 85–88)**
   Several mathematical expressions and algorithmic descriptions in the convolution sections contain technical inaccuracies that could cause confusion:
   * **Time-Domain vs. Transform-Domain Notation:** In Equation (2.3.1b) on PDF p. 21 (Thesis p. 10) and Equation (9.2.2a) on PDF p. 85 (Thesis p. 74), the convolution theorem is written as:
     $$y(n) = x(n) * h(n) = X(z) \cdot H(z)$$
     Equating a discrete-time signal $y(n)$ directly to a $z$-domain product $X(z) \cdot H(z)$ without an inverse transform operator ($\mathcal{Z}^{-1}\{\cdot\}$) mixes domains. In addition, Section 9.2.2 uses $X(z) \cdot H(z)$ to introduce Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT/FFT) frequency-domain multiplication, whereas $z$ is the $z$-transform variable (as defined in Section 9.4.1.4).
   * **Overlap-and-Add vs. Overlap-and-Save Sample Counts (Sections 9.2.3.1 & 9.2.3.2, PDF pp. 86–87):**
     * In Section 9.2.3.1 (PDF p. 87, Thesis p. 76), the text states: *"Once the separate convolutions have been executed, the resulting outputs are summed with overlapping by the input section length, N, minus one sample."* In general Overlap-Add convolution with input block length $L$ and impulse response length $M$, each convolved block has length $L + M - 1$ and consecutive blocks overlap by $M - 1$ samples (the RIR length minus one, not the input block length minus one; the two quantities only coincide in the worked example because both the input block length and RIR length are chosen equal to 3).
     * In Section 9.2.3.2 (PDF p. 87, Thesis p. 76), the text states: *"When the input blocks are being processed, the first $(N_k / 2) - 1$ samples are discarded..."* where $N_k$ is not previously defined. In general Overlap-Save, the number of circular-convolution alias samples discarded per block is $M - 1$ (where $M$ is the RIR length); this only equals $(N_k / 2) - 1$ in the special case where the FFT block length $N_k$ is chosen to be $2M$.
   * **FIR Convolution Summation Bounds and Complexity Wording (Sections 9.2.1 & 9.2.2, PDF pp. 85–86):**
     * In Equation (9.2.1a) (PDF p. 85), the summation upper limit is written as $N-1$, whereas the text defines the input length as $N(x)$ and the RIR length as $N(h)$, and Figure 9.2.1 (PDF p. 86) uses filter length $N_h$.
     * On PDF p. 85, convolving a 1-second input ($N(x) = 44,100$) with a 2-second RIR ($N(h) = 88,200$) at $44.1\text{ kHz}$ is stated to require *"~3.89 billion operations."* Because $44,100 \times 88,200 \approx 3.89 \times 10^9$, clarifying that this count refers to multiply-accumulate (MAC) pairs (or multiplications alone, with $\sim 7.78 \times 10^9$ total multiplications and additions) would make the arithmetic transparent.
     * On PDF p. 86, the phrasing *"This simplification requires two $N(x) + N(h) - 1$ FFTs ... and one $N(x) + N(h) - 1$ IDFTs"* can be misread as performing $2(N(x)+N(h)-1)$ separate FFTs rather than two FFTs and one IDFT *of length* $N(x) + N(h) - 1$.

### [Fractal Geometry, Higuchi Algorithm & Prior Mixing Time Predictors] (Pages: 31-48)

1. **Mathematical Formulation of the Higuchi Fractal Dimension Algorithm (Section 3.1.2, Pages 24 & 26 / PDF Pages 35 & 37):**
   There are several mathematical and notational inconsistencies in the presentation of the Higuchi algorithm that represent a potential concern for reproducibility if a reader relies on the equations in the text rather than the MATLAB implementation (`hfd.m`) provided in Appendix 9.1.3 (Pages 55–56 / PDF Pages 66–67):
   - **Omission of the $\frac{1}{k}$ Averaging Factor and Incorrect Summation Limit in $L(k)$ (Page 26 / PDF Page 37):** The text states that *"The length of the portion $L(k)$ for the time interval $k$ is computed as the mean of the $k$ values, for $m = 1, 2, \dots, k$,"* yet the accompanying inline equation defines $L(k)$ purely as a summation up to $M$ without dividing by $k$:
     $$L(k) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} L_m(k)$$
     To represent the mean across the $k$ sub-series lengths—and to match the MATLAB implementation in Appendix 9.1.3 (Page 56 / PDF Page 67, `Lk(1,k)=sum(Lmk(1:k,k))/k;`)—the upper summation limit should be $k$ rather than $M$, and the sum must be divided by $k$:
     $$L(k) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{m=1}^{k} L_m(k)$$
     Without the $\frac{1}{k}$ factor, $L(k)$ scales as $k^{1-D}$ rather than $k^{-D}$, shifting the log-log linear regression slope by $1.0$ and invalidating the resulting fractal dimension estimate.
   - **Regression Slope Sign Convention (Page 26 / PDF Page 37):** The text states that if $L(k) \propto k^{-D}$, then *"if $L(k)$ is plotted against $k = 1, \dots, k_{\max}$, on a double logarithmic scale, the points should fall on a straight line with a slope equal to $-D$. The least-square linear best fitting procedure is applied to the graph of $\ln(L(k))$ vs. $\ln(1/k)$. The coefficient of linear regression of the plot of $\ln(L(k))$ versus $\ln(1/k)$ is taken as an estimate of the fractal dimension of the portion."* Because $\ln(1/k) = -\ln(k)$, if $L(k) \propto k^{-D} = (1/k)^D$, plotting $\ln(L(k))$ against $\ln(k)$ yields a slope of $-D$, whereas plotting $\ln(L(k))$ against $\ln(1/k)$ yields a positive slope of $+D$ (which matches `lnk=log(1./[1:kmax]); b=polyfit(lnk,lnLk,1); xhfd=b(1);` in Appendix 9.1.3). Clarifying which horizontal axis corresponds to $-D$ versus $+D$ would prevent sign confusion.
   - **Conflation of Integer Floor (Gaussian) Notation and Absolute Value Bars, and Variable Switching (Pages 24 & 26 / PDF Pages 35 & 37):** In Equation (3.1.2a) on Page 24 (PDF Page 35), the time-series variable switches mid-equation from $y$ to $x$ at the final term: $y_m^k = \{y(m), y(m+k), y(m+2k), \dots, x(m+Mk)\}$ (where $x$ should be $y$). Furthermore, below Equation (3.1.2a), the upper summation index is defined as $M = |(N-m)/k|$, stating *"where $| \ |$ denotes the magnitude,"* whereas on Page 26 (PDF Page 37) the text states *"and $| \ |$ denotes Gaussian notation in Equation 3.1.2b."* In Higuchi (1988), Gauss's bracket notation $[ \cdot ]$ (the integer-part or floor function $\lfloor (N-m)/k \rfloor$, implemented as `fix((N-m)/k)` in `hfd.m`) is required so that the upper summation limit $M$ is an integer, whereas the vertical bars $| \cdot |$ inside the summation of Equation (3.1.2b) denote the absolute amplitude difference $|y(m+ik) - y(m+(i-1)k)|$. Using distinct symbols (such as $\lfloor \cdot \rfloor$ for the floor operation and $|\cdot|$ for absolute value) would eliminate this ambiguity.
   - **Interval Time $k$ Progression Formula (Page 26 / PDF Page 37):** For $k > 4$, the formula for interval times is written linearly as $k = [2(j-1)/4]$ for $j = 11, 12, 13, \dots$ (which evaluates to $5, 5.5, 6, \dots$). In Higuchi (1988), $(j-1)/4$ is an exponent in an exponential power-of-two scaling: $k = \lfloor 2^{(j-1)/4} \rfloor$ (yielding $k = 5, 6, 8, \dots$). Additionally, it is worth noting that the MATLAB implementation `hfd.m` used in this study (Page 55 / PDF Page 66) defaults to `kmax=5` and iterates linearly via `for k=1:kmax`.

2. **Dimensional and Unit Inconsistencies in Model-Based Mixing Time Predictors (Section 4.1, Pages 27–28 / PDF Pages 38–39):**
   - **Mean Free Path Units ($l_m$) (Page 28 / PDF Page 39):** The text states: *"The equation for $l_m$ is written as $l_m = 4\frac{V}{S}\text{ (ms)}$, where $S$ is the total surface area of the room ($\text{m}^2$)."* Because $V$ is in $\text{m}^3$ and $S$ is in $\text{m}^2$, the mean free path $l_m = \frac{4V}{S}$ is a spatial distance with units of metres ($\text{m}$), not milliseconds ($\text{ms}$). 5. **Weierstrass Scaling Identity and Summation Bounds (Section 3.1, Pages 20–21 / PDF Pages 31–32):**

   $$w(\beta t) = \sum_{k=0}^{M} \cos(\beta^{k+1} t) = \sum_{k=1}^{M+1} \cos(\beta^k t)$$
   For a finite $M$, $\sum_{k=1}^{M+1} \cos(\beta^k t)$ differs from $w(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{M} \cos(\beta^k t)$ by the boundary terms $\cos(\beta^{M+1}t) - \cos(t)$, and for an infinite one-sided series ($k \ge 0$), setting $\gamma = 1$ causes $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \cos(\beta^k t)$ to diverge. Weierstrass's continuous nowhere-differentiable function requires $0 < \gamma < 1$ (with $\beta\gamma > 1$), whereas Schroeder's [1989] auditory paradox uses a finite band-limited sum $M$ (or doubly infinite series over audible frequencies) to approximate scale invariance. Clarifying the summation bounds and amplitude scaling parameter $\gamma$ would make this mathematical motivation more rigorous.

#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Methodological Discrepancies in Baseline Comparisons: Synthetic Monaural ISM RIRs vs. Measured Binaural RIRs and Sample Size Mismatch ($n=8$ vs. $n=9$) (Sections 5.1, 6.2 & Table 6.3, pp. 49–51, 56–58 [thesis pp. 38–40, 45–47])**
   - **Synthetic Monaural Simulation vs. Measured Binaural Ground Truth (Potential Methodological Concern):** In Lindau et al. (2010), the ground-truth perceptual mixing times ($t_{\text{mp50\%}}$) and all competing empirical signal-based baselines (Abel & Huang, Hidaka et al., Stewart & Sandler, Defrance et al.; summarized in Table 6.3) were evaluated on actual *measured* Binaural Room Impulse Responses (BRIRs) captured in physical rooms using the FABIAN head and torso simulator. Real rooms contain furniture and audience seating (noted in Section 4.3.1.1), non-specular surface scattering, wave diffraction, frequency-dependent boundary and air absorption, transducer phase responses, and ambient background noise floors—all of which directly accelerate reflection mixing and govern time-series waveform complexity. In contrast, the proposed Fractal Dimension (FD) method is evaluated exclusively on idealized, noise-free, monaural Image-Source Method (ISM) simulations of empty rectangular enclosures with uniform, frequency-independent wall reflection coefficients ($\beta$). Furthermore, comparing Figure 2.2c (p. 19 [thesis p. 8]) and Figure 5.1b (p. 51 [thesis p. 40]) against `sroom.m` (Appendix 9.1.4.2, p. 69 [thesis p. 58]) reveals an ambiguity in signal pre-processing: `sroom.m` applies a 3rd-order Butterworth high-pass filter (`[B,A] = BUTTER(3,.01,'high'); ht=filter(B,A,ht);`, consistent with Allen and Berkley's original routine to remove DC offset) that introduces bipolar filter ringing around each impulse, whereas Figures 2.2c and 5.1b explicitly describe and plot strictly non-negative (unipolar/rectified) impulse amplitudes (*"an idealised SPL profile with only positive values"*). Either the high-pass filter lines in `sroom.m` were bypassed/commented out when generating the RIRs, or a rectification step (`abs(ht)`) was applied prior to plotting and FD analysis. Because time-domain curve-length estimators like the Higuchi algorithm compute absolute sample-to-sample differences $|x(m+ik)-x(m+(i-1)k)|$ and are highly sensitive to zero-crossings, bipolar oscillations, and additive noise floors, applying the estimator to a noise-free, unipolar synthetic pulse train yields a different curve-length growth profile than applying it to a real, zero-mean bipolar measured RIR. Clarifying the exact signal representation passed to `HMW.m` and evaluating the proposed FD algorithm directly on the measured BRIRs from Lindau et al. (or evaluating the competing empirical baselines on the same ISM RIRs) is needed to establish whether the reported $R^2 = 93.49\%$ correlation generalizes to real-world acoustic measurements.
   - **Dataset Subset Mismatch ($n=8$ vs. $n=9$ Rooms) in Table 6.3 (Definitive Statistical Discrepancy):** As stated in Chapter 5 (p. 49 [thesis p. 38]), Room 9 (JC church, $V = 7417\text{ m}^3$, $RT = 2.36\text{ s}$) was excluded from the evaluation because its non-shoebox geometry cannot be modelled by the rectangular ISM, leaving $n = 8$ rooms. (2010) for all model-based and empirical baselines. Because $R^2$ on small samples is highly sensitive to removing a single high-leverage data point—particularly the most reverberant and geometrically complex room in the dataset—comparing 8-room correlations directly against 9-room correlations is not an apples-to-apples statistical benchmark. At minimum, the baseline geometric predictors ($V/S$, $\sqrt{V}$, $V$, $S$, and $RT$) should be recomputed on the identical 8-room subset for a consistent comparison.

3. **Anomalous Fractal Dimension Estimates Exceeding the Theoretical Upper Bound of 2.0 (Sections 5.2 & 6.1, Figures 5.2–5.3, and Table 6.1a, pp. 50–55 [thesis pp. 39–44])**
   - **Violation of Theoretical Planar Curve Bounds (Definitive Mathematical & Algorithmic Issue):** As stated in Section 3.1.1 (p. 33 [thesis p. 22]) and illustrated in Figures 3.1.2a–b (p. 36 [thesis p. 25]), the fractal dimension of a shape exceeds its topological dimension by an amount strictly less than one; thus, for a 1D time-series waveform embedded in a 2D plane (amplitude vs. time), the theoretical fractal dimension is strictly bounded within the interval $[1.0, 2.0]$. However, in Figures 5.2 and 5.3 (pp. 52–53 [thesis pp. 41–42]), the smoothed FD profile fluctuates above $2.0$ (peaking above $2.1$), and in Table 6.1a (p. 55 [thesis p. 44]), the reported mean FD values over the last 10% of the RIR for Rooms 1, 2, and 3 exceed $2.0$ ($2.0364$, $2.0450$, and $2.0250$, respectively, with Criterion I and II thresholds also exceeding $2.0$).
   - **Technical Diagnosis:** Inspecting the Higuchi algorithm implementation (`hfd.m`, Appendix 9.1.3, pp. 66–67 [thesis pp. 55–56]) alongside the moving-window wrapper (`HMW.m`, Appendix 9.1.2, pp. 65–66 [thesis pp. 54–55], where `TraceSeg = k(i:(i+y),j)` with $y=50$ extracts a short window of $N=51$ samples and single-argument call `l(i,j) = hfd(TraceSeg)` triggers the default hyperparameter `kmax = 5`) explains why this overshoot occurs. For discrete, unsmoothed impulse trains where reflection arrivals are rounded to integer sample indices (`delp(i)=round(sqrt(delsq))` in `sroom.m`, p. 68 [thesis p. 57]), adjacent samples alternate rapidly between zero and isolated spikes. Over a 51-sample window, the single-step finite difference $|x(m+ik) - x(m+(i-1)k)|$ at $k=1$ combined with the finite-sample normalization factor `Ng = (N-1)/(fix((N-m)/k)*k)` is disproportionately larger than at coarser intervals $k \in \{2,\dots,5\}$ (where decimated sub-series can miss isolated impulses or alias across sharp impulse peaks), causing $L(k)$ to drop faster than $k^{-2}$ and the ordinary least-squares slope of $\ln(L(k))$ vs. $\ln(1/k)$ (`b(1)`) to overshoot the continuous geometric upper bound of $2.0$. Chapters 6 and 7 should explicitly acknowledge that FD values above $2.0$ are finite-sample numerical estimation artefacts on short discrete impulse sequences rather than true geometric fractal dimensions exceeding $2.0$, and discuss why the small rooms (Rooms 1–3) exhibit higher asymptotic FD estimates ($>2.02$) than the larger rooms (Rooms 4–8, $<1.99$).

#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Speed of Sound Scaling and Unit Conversion Discrepancies in `sroomguicbs.m` (Section 9.1.5.2, Document p. 70 / PDF p. 81):**
   A critical potential concern exists in how physical spatial dimensions (`roomx`, `roomy`, `roomz`) and source/receiver coordinates (`sx`, `sy`, `sz`, `rx`, `ry`, `rz`) are converted into discrete sample delays (`rl`, `r0`, `r`) under the `'computeresponse'` callback in `sroomguicbs.m`:
   ```matlab
   if units==1 
       factor=1;
   elseif units==2
       factor=3.28;
   end
   rl(1)=roomx*fs*factor/1000;
   ```
   (and identically for `rl(2)`, `rl(3)`, `r0`, and `r`), where `units == 1` corresponds to `'metres'` (as shown selected in the GUI in Figure 5.1a, Document p. 40 / PDF p. 51) and `units == 2` corresponds to `'feet'`. This implementation introduces two compounding mathematical and physical scaling issues:
   - **Implicit Speed of Sound ($c = 1000\text{ m/s}$ vs. $c \approx 343\text{ m/s}$):** In Allen and Berkley's (1979) original Fortran code, spatial dimensions were specified in imperial units (feet) and divided by `1000` to model an approximate speed of sound in air of $c \approx 1000\text{ ft/s}$ ($\approx 304.8\text{ m/s}$, or more precisely $c \approx 1125\text{ ft/s}$ for $343\text{ m/s}$), where sample delay is given by $N_{\text{samples}} = \frac{d \cdot f_s}{c}$. Setting `factor = 1` for metres (`units == 1`) while retaining the `/ 1000` divisor computes the sample delay as $\text{distance (m)} \times f_s / 1000$, which implicitly models an acoustic medium with a speed of sound of $c = 1000\text{ m/s}$—nearly three times faster than the actual speed of sound in air ($\approx 343\text{ m/s}$). Consequently, all reflection arrival times and inter-reflection spacings in the simulated Room Impulse Responses (RIRs) are compressed in time by a factor of roughly $1000 / 343 \approx 2.915$, and the theoretical reflection density $\frac{dN}{dt} \propto c^3$ is inflated by a factor of $(2.915)^3 \approx 24.8$. For example, in Room 1, the direct path distance between source $(x, 2, 1.9)\text{ m}$ and receiver $(x, 1, 1.9)\text{ m}$ is $1.0\text{ m}$, which physically arrives at $\approx 2.92\text{ ms}$ ($\approx 129\text{ samples}$ at $44.1\text{ kHz}$), whereas the formula places it at $1.0\text{ ms}$ ($44\text{ samples}$). Notably, in Equation 6.2 and Table 6.2 (Document pp. 45–46 / PDF pp. 56–57), the linear regression slope relating Criterion III sample numbers to the empirical perceptual mixing time $t_{\text{mp50\%}}$ is $0.3197$ (whose reciprocal is $3.13$, and $343 / 1000 = 0.343$). This indicates that the empirical regression coefficient is largely compensating for the compressed time scale introduced by using $c = 1000\text{ m/s}$ instead of $c \approx 343\text{ m/s}$ in the Image-Source Method (ISM) simulation.
   - **Inverted Imperial-to-Metric Conversion Factor:** Because $1\text{ m} \approx 3.28084\text{ ft}$, if the base denominator `1000` assumes feet ($c \approx 1000\text{ ft/s}$), then converting an input specified in metres (`units == 1`) into feet requires multiplying by `3.28084` (`factor = 3.28084`, yielding an effective speed of sound of $\frac{1000}{3.28084} \approx 304.8\text{ m/s}$), whereas an input already in feet (`units == 2`) requires `factor = 1`. Conversely, if the denominator were corrected to `343` for metres, converting from feet to metres would require dividing by `3.28084` (`factor = 1 / 3.28084`), not multiplying. Assigning `factor = 1` to metres and `factor = 3.28` to feet inverts the physical unit relationship (causing a $10\text{ ft}$ dimension to be treated as $32.8\text{ ft}$). Replacing `factor/1000` with explicit division by the physical speed of sound ($c \approx 343\text{ m/s}$ for metres and $c \approx 1125\text{ ft/s}$ for feet) is recommended to ensure physical time-scale accuracy.

2. **Unassigned Output Argument, Off-by-One Window Length, and Loop Termination Bugs in `HMW.m` (Section 9.1.2, Document pp. 54–55 / PDF pp. 65–66):**
   A line-by-line audit of `HMW.m` reveals four implementation issues that affect code execution, edge-of-array behavior, and reproducibility:
   - **Unassigned Return Variable (`FD`):** The function signature is declared as `function [FD] = HMW(x,y)`, but the return variable `FD` is never assigned anywhere in the function body. Instead, the raw Higuchi FD values are stored in `l(i,j)` and the smoothed profile is stored in `sl = smooth(l,200)`. Calling `FD = HMW(x, 50)` in MATLAB will throw an `Output argument "FD" (and maybe others) not assigned during call to "HMW"` error unless the user modifies the script or extracts `sl` manually from the workspace.
   - **Off-by-One Window Slicing (`y + 1` Samples):** The sliding window is extracted via `TraceSeg = k(i:(i+y),j);`. Because MATLAB 1-based colon indexing is inclusive at both bounds, `i:(i+y)` extracts `y + 1` samples (i.e., $51\text{ samples}$ when called with $y = 50$), rather than the $50\text{-sample}$ window stated in Section 5.2 (Document p. 39 / PDF p. 50). To extract a window of exact length $y$, the slice should be `k(i:(i+y-1),j)`.
   - **Premature and Misplaced Loop Termination Condition:** The script pads the end of the input RIR `x` (of length $L$) with $y$ zeros (`z = zeros(y,m); k = cat(1,x,z);`, so `length(k)` is $L + y$) so that the sliding window can traverse the full length of `x`. However, inside the loop, `TraceSeg = k(i:(i+y),j);` is executed *before* the boundary check:
     ```matlab
     if length(TraceSeg) + i + 1 > length(k) %Break if end of waveform reached 
         break
     end
     ```
     Because `length(TraceSeg)` is `y + 1` and `length(k)` is `length(x) + y`, this condition triggers when `(y + 1) + i + 1 > length(x) + y`, which simplifies to `i > length(x) - 2`. Thus, the loop terminates two samples short of the original signal length `length(x)`, leaving the final two samples of `x` unanalyzed and rendering the appended zero-padding `z` largely unused (moreover, placing the boundary check after indexing `k(i:(i+y),j)` would throw an out-of-bounds error if `i + y` ever exceeded `length(k)`). Iterating `for i = 1:length(x)` with `TraceSeg = k(i:(i+y-1),j);` resolves both the off-by-one window length and the premature termination without needing the `if ... break` check.
   - **Multi-Channel Smoothing Behavior:** If a multi-channel RIR (`m > 1`) is passed to `HMW.m`, `sl = smooth(l,200)` is called inside the outer `for j=1:m` loop on the entire 2D matrix `l`. In MATLAB's Curve Fitting Toolbox, `smooth` vectorizes a matrix argument column-by-column into a single column vector rather than smoothing each channel independently; it should be indexed column-wise and assigned to the output variable as `FD(:,j) = smooth(l(:,j), 200);`.

3. **Filename and Function Name Mismatch Between `ithimage.m` and `lthimage` (Table of Contents, Document p. v / PDF p. 6; Section 9.1.4.1, Document p. 56 / PDF p. 67):**
   Sub-section 9.1.4.1 (and its Table of Contents entry on PDF p. 6) is titled `9.1.4.1 ithimage.m` (starting with an `i`), whereas the function definition in the code (`function delp=lthimage(dr,dr0,rl,nr)`) and its caller inside `sroom.m` on Document p. 57 / PDF p. 68 (`delp=lthimage(r,r0,rl,nr);`) spell the routine `lthimage` (starting with a lowercase `L`, matching Allen & Berkley's Fortran subroutine `LTHIMAGE` for the $l$-th image).
