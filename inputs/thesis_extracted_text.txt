ABSTRACT 
 
 
 
Hybrid artificial reverberation is a form of artificial reverberation that aims to take 
advantage of the acoustic realism of reverberation created through convolution with room 
impulse responses and the efficiency and versatility of reverberation created using 
filterbanks. In order to make best use of each type of reverberation, the point at which a 
room impulse response transitions from early to late reflections (the mixing time) must be 
known. 
Since the mid-20th century, fractal geometry has provided a new language by which to 
describe and explore the mechanism of many natural and physical processes. It has 
facilitated advances in fields ranging from telecommunications to human-integrated 
technologies. 
 In this project, the Higuchi algorithm for calculating fractal dimension was used to 
produce fractal dimension profiles for eight room impulse responses. These room impulse 
responses were produced using Allen and Berkley’s image-source method and were 
modelled on shoebox shaped rooms from a study looking at the perceptual evaluation of 
mixing time. The resulting fractal dimension profiles for the eight room impulse 
responses were analysed in terms of the length of time taken for the profile to reach a 
threshold determined by one of four different criteria, and the resulting values compared 
to results from the perceptual evaluation of the mixing time study. Through this analysis 
an equation calculating the appropriate mixing time for use in hybrid artificial 
reverberation was established. This equation produced an explained variance of 93.49% 
with the results for the perceptual evaluation of mixing time. 
 The results from this study show that analysis of the fractal dimension profile of room 
impulse responses offers a method for calculating the appropriate mixing time for hybrid 
artificial reverberation that is both simple in its implementation and of greater accuracy to 
previous methods.

1 INTRODUCTION 
 
 
  
 
The design of Virtual Acoustic Environments (VAE) of increasing sophistication is 
continually being sought to be implemented in conjunction with 3D visual environments. 
This has become increasingly important in various fields of research, as well as for 
special effects in the film industry and in the ever more sophisticated video game 
industry. As these 3D environments increase in visual sophistication, there is an 
expectation to provide an equally realistic acoustic environment. One factor that must be 
considered when constructing a VAE is the minimisation of the processing power 
required to create it. One way this can be achieved is through the use of hybrid artificial 
reverberation (HAR) [Primavera et al, 2010]. HAR aims to exploit the knowledge of a 
room’s reflection profile (which is known as its room impulse response, RIR), and the 
limit of human perception of distinct reflections within a particular acoustic environment.  
 An RIR comprises three parts: The direct sound (the initial acoustic input into the room), 
the early reflections (which provide the listener with information on the geometry of the 
physical space) and the late reflections (the point at which the listener can no longer 
differentiate between reflections). The point at which the early reflections transition into 
the later reflections can either be described in terms of the mathematical behaviour of the 
system, known as the mixing time (tmix) or in terms of how the auditory system perceives 
the behaviour of the system, known as the perceptual mixing time (tmp). HAR uses a 
convolution of a recorded or mathematically modelled RIR with an input sound to 
generate the early reflections, and then generates the late reflections algorithmically using 
filters [Primavera et al, 2010; Murphy & Stewart, 2007]. The convolution of an input 
sound with an RIR produces the best possible reproduction of a room’s reflection profile 
but suffers from being computationally expensive. Algorithmically generated 
reverberation is computationally inexpensive but can produce unnatural audible artefacts. 
To make the most of HAR by maximising sound quality while minimising computational 
load, it is vital that a method exists so that the tmix can be precisely calculated.

Fractals are a geometric phenomenon regularly observed in nature, the mathematics of 
which have been exploited since the latter half of the 20th century to explain and explore 
a number of different of natural systems. Fractals are characterised as rough or 
fragmented shapes that are self-similar at various levels of magnification, and can be 
described by a dimension that is a non-integer value greater than its topological 
dimension. The fractal dimension (FD) of a fractal is a statistical quantity used to 
describe how completely that fractal fills space as the level of magnification used to view 
it is varied, and is a measure of how complicated the shape is [Mandelbrot, 1982]. A 
mathematical algorithm can be used for estimating the FD of real-world fractal systems 
as, invariably, the fractal will not be self-similar at all levels of magnification. The 
estimated FD describes how complex the fractal is, and hence how much space it appears 
to fill within a specific magnification range [Higuchi, 1988; Raghavendra & Dutt, 2010]. 
 Over the past decade or so, the mathematical understanding of fractal systems has been 
exploited on a number of fronts; it has been used to vastly improve telecommunication 
systems and minimise the size of antennae [Hohlfield, 1999], to create computer-based 
musical instrument recognition systems [Gunasekaran & Revathy 2008], and also to 
detect changes in neural activity in the brain [Raghavendra & Dutt 2009]. Recently it has 
also been observed that how the eye tracks images on a screen, the paths animals take 
when foraging for food, and the aesthetic appeal of objects have a strict fractal 
dependence. In addition to this, images of a FD of around 1.5 can reduce the 
physiological response to stress by up to 60%, with functional magnetic resonance 
imaging (fMRI) experiments showing that these mid-range FD images activate distinct 
regions of the brain associated with the regulation of emotions such as happiness [Taylor, 
2011].  
 These examples highlight the pervasive and fundamental role fractal systems play in our 
natural and physical environment. More specifically it exposes the fractal dependence on 
a number of cognitive systems within the human brain and opens up the possibility that 
the auditory perception of our environment may share a similar dependence. 
 
For this investigation, the results from a paper detailing a study that evaluated tmp for a 
range of typical acoustic environments [Lindau, 2010], were compared with time-varying 
fractal dimension profiles of modelled RIRs for these environments, with the intention of 
developing a method for predicting tmp by analysis of a time-varying FD profile of an 
RIR. The paper also details a comprehensive, comparative study of previous methods for

 The modelled RIRs used for this investigation were created using the image-source 
method (ISM) [Allen & Berkley, 1979], which was able to make use of values provided

each room.  The study examined nine rooms ranging in volume from 216m3 to 8500m3,

0.39seconds to 2.08seconds. From a series of listening tests these rooms were found to 
have mean perceptual mixing times (tmp50%) ranging from 37.5ms to 97.8ms. Eight of the 
nine rooms from this study were modelled to produce RIRs (see Chapter 5 for 
disqualification criteria of the ninth room). 
  A number of different algorithms are available for calculating the FD of real world 
systems. In this investigation the Higuchi algorithm was used due to its demonstrated 
accuracy against fractals of known FD, and its ability to handle small data sets [Higuchi, 
1988; Raghavendra & Dutt, 2010]. FD profiles for the modelled RIRs were obtained 
using a ‘moving-window’ function written in MATLAB (HMW.m, see Appendix 1.2 for 
MATLAB code). This function works by travelling along the RIR one sample at a time, 
applying the Higuchi Algorithm1 (hfd.m, see Appendix 1.3) to calculate the FD over the 
length of the window. This function produces a profile of how the FD of each RIR 
changes over time.  
  
 After producing FD profiles for the eight rooms, they were then individually compared 
to their respective tmp50% from the paper. This was achieved by proposing a set of criteria 
that defined when the FD profiles had reached a specific state. Each of these criteria was 
applied to each profile separately and the results plotted against tmp50% and tested for 
correlation.

2 OVERVIEW OF REVERBERATION 
 
 
 
 Reverberation occurs in a confined space after a sound is produced at some point within 
the space, due to reflections of the sound wave off of surfaces. The energy within the 
sound wave will be partially reflected depending on the absorption properties of surface 
material. Provided there is a direct path between the sound source and the listener, after a 
short initial period of time the listener will hear the direct sound followed by the first 
reflections from the surfaces. These initial reflections are known as the early reflections 
and provide the listener with details of the geometry of the room and the location of the 
source. Eventually the density of the reflections will be such that the listener will no 
longer be able to distinguish between them. These reflections are known as the late 
reflections, or the reverberation tail, and provide the listener with a sense of the 
spaciousness of the room, as well as defining the room’s unique timbre or ‘sound’. The 
combination of these early and late reflections is known as reverberation and the point at 
which the early reflections give way to the late reflections is known as the mixing time. 
 The reflection profile of a room is generally represented as a RIR. This RIR shows the 
variation in sound pressure level (SPL) over time for some point in the enclosed space 
after a sound has been produced. The amount of reverberation a space will produce is 
described as the time it takes the SPL to reduce by 60dB (which is equivalent to 
1/1,000,000th) compared to the original level. It can be calculated from Sabine’s 
reverberation equation [Sabine, 1993], or manually using a loud, short impulse, such as a 
gunshot, and a recording device. Due to the absorption characteristics of the surface 
materials, the reverberation time (RT) of an enclosed space will be frequency dependant, 
so RT is often measured over frequency bands of 1/6, 1/3 or single octaves [Giesbrecht et 
al, 2009].

2.2 Room Impulse Response 
 
 A room impulse response (RIR) describes the variation in the SPL over time from the 
instant a sound is produced in an enclosed space from the perspective of a single point in 
that space. A RIR is divided into three main sections; the direct sound, the early 
reflections and late reflections (or reverberation tail). A hypothetical RIR profile is 
demonstrated in Figure 2.2a, showing the SPL variation at one point in space; from the 
direct sound to the early reflections and finally the indistinguishable late reflections. The 
characteristics of a RIR are determined by the source and receiver positions, the 
absorption properties of surface materials, geometry of the room, and the extent of 
specular diffusion due to uneven surfaces or objects in the room.  
 
There are a number of methods available to procure a RIR of an acoustic space [Stan et 
al, 2002]. The basic principle of all these methods involves the input of a known and 
reproducible signal into an acoustic environment and a measurement taken of the output 
produced by the environment.

2.2.1 Mixing Time 
 
The point at which the reflections within a room can be defined as the late reflections 
may be determined by the statistical behaviour of the reflections in relation to each other 
in the room. Once the reflections have been determined to have statistically lost any 
memory of their initial state, they are said to be diffuse or mixed. The time from the 
initial input into the system to this state of statistical independence is defined as the 
mixing time, tmix.  
 Another definition for the late reflections is the point at which no extra, perceptual 
information from reflections can be obtained at one location over any other in the room. 
This is known as the perceptual mixing time, tmp. This point will occur before tmix due to 
auditory and cognitive masking effects [Meesawat & Hammershoi, 2003]. There are a 
number of factors that will influence the time taken to reach tmix or tmp, such as a room’s 
volume, a large variance in surface absorption coefficients (i.e., rooms with glass 
windows and floor carpeting), and rooms that are coupled or irregular in shape. Rooms 
where there is a lack of irregular objects to increase diffusion of the reflections will also 
increase the time taken to reach tmix or tmp. Mixing times will also be frequency dependant 
due to the absorption characteristics of surface materials [Lindau 2010].

2.2.2 Diffusion of Reflections in a Room 
 
In an ideal reflective room the sound energy will diffuse evenly and quadratically through 
the room’s space over time. This will be met with a quadratic increase in density of 
reflections [Kutruff, 2000]. In the ideal reflective room a uniform angular distribution of 
sound energy flux and a constant acoustical energy will be observed at any point in the 
room. However, due to the absorption properties of surface materials, there will be a net 
loss of energy towards the surfaces, biasing the reflection diffusion [Polack, 1992]. Also, 
due to even slight anomalies on surfaces there will be a tendency towards an ever-
increasing level of diffusion due to specular reflections, thus the mixing of reflections in a 
real room can never be perfectly diffuse or stable. 
 
2.3 Digital Reverberation 
 
Digital reverberation can be achieved by convolving an input signal with a real or 
simulated RIR, or by processing a signal through a network of digital filters. The method 
of using a simulated RIR, or processing a signal using filters, is known as artificial digital 
reverberation. The following sections of this chapter will deal with the fundamentals of 
digital reverberation.

2.4 Artificial Digital Reverberation 
 
 The objective of artificial digital reverberation (ADR) is to simulate the reverberation of 
real acoustic environments and apply this to acoustic recordings in the digital domain. 
ADR is achieved by either convolving an input signal with a simulated RIR or by 
processing an input signal using an algorithm comprising a network of digital filters.  
Each method has its own benefits and drawbacks. 
 Convolving a signal with a simulated RIR produces a high quality reproduction of a 
room’s characteristics but can be computationally expensive. Convolution also limits 
certain parameters such as source and receiver positions.  
 Processing an input signal using digital filters produces a series of delayed and 
attenuated repetitions of the input signal (in the case of sound this would produce 
echoes). This method has the advantage of being highly flexible in regards to the type of 
sound produced and often allows for control of a variety of input parameters [Murphy & 
Stewart, 2007]. One criticism of this method is its lack of authenticity when compared to 
the characteristics of real-world acoustic environments [Jot, 1997; Murphy & Stewart, 
2007].

2.4.1 Image Source Method 
 
 The image-source method (ISM) devised by Allen and Berkley [Allen & Berkley, 1979] 
is a method for generating simulated room impulse responses (RIRs). This method 
generates an RIR with source and receiver positions by imagining a number of image-
sources spaced out on an infinite grid of mirror rooms expanding in all three dimensions. 
Figure 2.4.1 shows a number of image-sources, Ii, in the x-y plane with the original 
source, S, and receiver, R, positions. The contribution of each image source to the 
receiver is a replica of the original source signal delayed by a lag time, τ, and attenuated 
by an amplitude factor A. The mathematics for this method is described in detail in 
Appendix 3.1.  
 
2.4.2 Algorithmic Artificial Reverberation 
 
 The comb filter and all-pass filter are the basic filters from which complex algorithms 
have been created in an attempt to reproduce the decay characteristics of different 
acoustic environments. Both these filters are known as Infinite Impulse Response (IIR) 
filters. All digital filters are either Finite Impulse Response (FIR) or IIR filters, with IIRs 
being a more efficient form of filter.  An IIR is characterised by always containing a 
feedback element and its impulse response never completely decaying to zero. 
 In the following sections each filter will be briefly described, and how they contribute to 
the modification of a signal to produce an approximation of real acoustic environments is 
explained. See Appendix 5 for a detailed breakdown of the filters including background 
on different analysis techniques of digital filter algorithms.

2.4.3 Filter Implementation in Algorithmic Artificial Reverberation 
 
In order to approximate the behaviour of real RIRs, a number of configurations of all-
pass and comb filters have been proposed.  
 In 1962 Manfred Schroeder first proposed two digital artificial reverberator types 
[Schroeder, 1962]. The first was made up of a series of five all pass filters (see Figure 
2.4.3.1). The second comprised a combination of four comb filters in parallel followed by 
two all-pass filters in series (see Figure 2.4.3.2). Although the first reverberator type has a 
flat frequency response, it still suffers from transient ‘coloration’ of the output sound. A 
further problem with the first reverberator is that its amplitude does not display an 
exponential decay. The first reverberator type also lacks the build up in reflection density 
provided by the combination of all-pass and comb filters in the second reverberator. 
These problems are overcome in the second reverberator type. In this second type, the 
comb filters job is to recreate reflections between parallel walls and imitate the air 
absorption of the sound, with their frequency responses modelling the normal modes of 
the space as seen in a real RIR.

3 FRACTAL GEOMETRY 
 
 
 
Fractal Geometry is a modern addition to the mathematician’s toolbox that has allowed 
the accurate description of a broad range of natural processes. This new mathematical 
vocabulary has facilitated technical advances and new levels of academic understanding 
in fields ranging from telecommunications to human-integrated technology. 
 
Benoit B. Mandelbrot first coined the term ‘fractal’ in 1982 to describe “a rough or 
fragmented geometric shape that can be split into parts, each of which is (at least 
approximately) a reduced-size copy of the whole” [Mandelbrot, 1982]. The two defining 
aspects of a fractal are that it exhibits self-similarity at arbitrary levels of magnification, 
and that it can be described by a non-integer dimension whose value is greater than that 
of the its topological dimension. Mathematically, a fractal can be generated by repeated 
implementation (iteration) of an equation, which by its nature will result in recursive self- 
similarity. 
 
3.1 Development of Fractal Geometry 
  
The idea of self-similarity was first proposed in the 17th century by Gottried Liebnitz’ 
attempts to define the properties of a straight line. However, the basis of the mathematics 
of fractal geometry dates back to 1872 with Karl Weierstrauss’ demonstration that a 
function could be both continuous everywhere, but nowhere differentiable. Consider the

Weierstrauss’ mathematical description was followed up by a more graphical description

set of rules that are repeated ad infinitum. First a line segment is divided into three 
segments of equal length. An equilateral triangle is then constructed that has the middle 
segment from the original line segment as its base. Finally the line segment that forms the 
base of the triangle is removed. See Figure 3.1a for a depiction of Koch iterations

Following the work of Weierstrauss and Koch, investigations of iterated functions in the 
complex plane were carried out by Henri Poincaré, Felix Klein, Pierre Fatou and Gaston 
Julia (responsible for the Julia set) [Mandelbrot, 1982; Schroeder, 1989], who described a 
series of new fractal systems. Without the means to visualise this new set of unusual 
functions, mathematicians were unable to ascribe them any significance beyond mere 
curiosity. 
 
With the advent of modern computer technology, fractal geometry was released from the 
limitations of manual calculation, allowing the visualisation of rapid and numerous 
iterative calculations in the real and complex planes. By being able to visualise the results 
and evolution of millions of iterations of these fractal functions, the true importance of 
these curious functions and their uncanny resemblance to natural processes could finally 
be realised.  
 
 In the 1960’s, Benoit B. Mandelbrot started investigating self-similarity, publishing 
‘How Long is the Cost of Britain? Statistical Self-Similarity and Fractal Dimension’ 
[Mandelbrot, 1967]. Using computers to run large numbers of iterations on the different

5 METHOD 
 
 
 
It was intended that the fractal dimension (FD) of room impulse responses (RIRs) be 
investigated for correlation with known values for the perceptual mixing time (tmp) for a 
set of rooms. Lindau’s study was chosen for investigation because not only did the study 
provide values for tmp for a variety of acoustic environments of known dimensions and 
average absorption coefficients (αave) from which the rooms could be modelled, but it 
also presented results for the gamut of mixing time predictors against which the 
effectiveness of this method could be measured. 
 
To investigate how the FD of RIRs correlates with tmp, models for the first eight rooms 
from the Lindau study were created based on Allen & Berkley’s [1979] image-source 
method (ISM) in MATLAB4 [see Appendix 1 for code].  The ninth room from the study 
was excluded as it deviated from the typical shoebox shape (see Figure 4.3.1.1) and as 
such, is beyond the modelling capability of the ISM.  
 
 How the FD varied over the lifetime of a RIR was of great interest for this study. To 
obtain this FD ‘profile’ of a RIR, a function called HMW.m (see Appendix 1.2) was 
created in MATLAB that generated a moving window of a pre-defined sample length, 
which moves incrementally along the length of the RIR. As the moving window advances 
along the RIR, it implements the Higuchi algorithm (hfd.m – See appendix 1.3 for 
MATLAB code) for calculating the FD over the sample length, resulting in a profile 
showing how the FD of the RIR changes over its length. HMW.m was applied to the eight 
modelled RIRs from the Lindau study to produce eight FD profiles. 
 Following the production of the eight FD profiles, a mathematical analysis was carried 
out on each profile relating the behaviour of the profiles to the respective mean perceptual 
mixing times (tmp50%) from Lindau’s study. The results from this analysis were then 
compared against Lindau’s results for predictors of tmp50% to determine the benefit, if any,

5.1 Image Source Method 
 
The ISM implemented in MATLAB employed a re-coding of Fortran5 code (see 
Appendix 1.4.1 & 1.4.2 for MATLAB code) available in the appendix of Allen & 
Berkley’s original ISM paper [Allen & Berkley, 1979]. ISM allows for the input of 
source and receiver position (in Cartesian coordinates) as well as for individual reflection 
coefficients, β, for each wall, the sample frequency and length of the resultant RIR. 
Figure 5.1a shows a typical setup of the MATLAB GUI (see Appendix 1.5.1 & 1.5.2 for 
MATLAB code) to generate an RIR.  
 When generating the eight RIRs modelled on Lindau’s study a sample frequency of 
44.1kHz was chosen. Only an average absorption coefficient (αave) for the whole of each 
room was available from the Lindau study, so this value was converted to β and inputted 
as each wall’s coefficient. The receiver for each room was placed at length, x = (half the 
length of the original room), width, y = 1m, and height, z = 1.9m, where x, y, and z 
represent length, width and height respectively. The source for each room was placed at  
x = (half the length of the original room), y = 2m, and z = 1.9m. 
Figure 5.1b shows the RIR created using ISM, modelled on Room 1 from Lindau’s study. 
 
 
5.2 Fractal Dimension Profile of Room Impulse Responses 
 
The FD profiles for each RIR were created using the HMW.m function with a window 
length of 50 samples (~1ms). This value was chosen as it provided a highly detailed 
profile (see Figure 5.2) while being large enough for the Higuchi algorithm to work 
effectively (see Figure 3.1.2b). Before the FD profile is plotted it is smoothed with a 
moving window of 200 samples to remove noise. In Figure 5.2 the FD profile for the ISM 
modelled RIR for room 1 from Lindau’s study can be seen.

5.3 Analysis 
 
The eight FD profiles were investigated to see if there was a correlation between the 
shape of the profile and tmp50% from the Lindau study. It was presumed from diffusion 
theory (Section 2.2) that the RIR would be most diffuse towards its end. On this 
presumption the mean and standard deviation for the last 10% of each FD profile were 
calculated. Once these values had been established, four criteria relating to the FD 
profiles were created for investigating the relationship between the shape of the curves 
and tmp50%. Criterion I specified the sample number at which the magnitude of the FD 
profile first reaches the mean value calculated from the last 10% of its length. Criteria II, 
III & IV specified the sample number at which the magnitude of the FD profile first 
reaches the mean value calculated from the last 10% of its length minus 1, 2 and 3 times 
the standard deviation of the last 10% of its length, respectively. Figure 5.3 shows tmp50% 
and the thresholds for criteria I – IV on the FD profile for Room 1.

7 DISCUSSION & CONCLUSIONS 
 
 
 
In this investigation an analysis of the fractal dimension (FD) profile of room impulse 
responses (RIRs) was investigated as a potential tool for predicting the perceptual mixing 
time (tmp) of room impulse responses for use in hybrid artificial reverberation. RIRs were 
created from models of eight acoustic environments of shoebox shape, which were 
previously used in a study evaluating the performance of various model- and RIR- based 
predictors for tmp [Lindau et al., 2010]. These RIRs were created using the image source 
method [Allen & Berkley, 1979]. Fractal dimension profiles of each of these RIRs were 
generated using the Higuchi algorithm [Higuchi, 1988] for estimating FD. The eight FD 
profiles were compared to mean perceptual mixing time (tmp50%) values from the 
evaluation study, which had been obtained from listening tests, under four criteria. Under 
the boundaries of the third criterion a correlation between the FD profile and tmp50% was 
found, with an explained variance (R2) of 93.4% and a linear regression equation of the 
form, 
 
 
tmp50% = 0.3197· III + 325,     
(6.2) 
 
where III indicates the sample number at the point the FD profile first crosses the FD 
threshold imposed by Criterion III. 
 
 Methods for predicting the mixing time (tmix) that look at RIRs have possibly failed in 
the past [Abel & Huang, 2006; Hidaka, 2007; Stewart & Sandler, 2007; Defrance et al, 
2009] because by basing an analysis on the statistical behaviour of the reflections in the 
room, the behaviour (and limitations) of the human cognitive and auditory systems are 
not being fully taken into account.  Model-based methods for predicting tmp (see 
equations 4.3.2.1a-d) may have lacked accuracy, as they may not have considered all the 
factors contributing to the perception of mixing in a room. The results from this 
investigation suggest that the complexity of reflections in an acoustic environment, i.e., 

===== PAGE 60 =====
 
 
 
49 
the amount of similarity over time, might contribute to the perception of mixing in an 
acoustic environment.  
 This method for determining tmp offers a way to measure the complexity of an RIR, and, 
by the establishment of a criterion from experiment [Lindau et al., 2010] to equate human 
perception with the complexity of a RIR, provides a marked improvement over previous 
prediction methods [Lindau et al., 2010], with the added advantage that the FD profile of 
any room can easily and quickly be derived from a measured or modelled RIR.  
 
7.1 Further Work 
 
 This approach to predicting the perceptual mixing time could be advanced with a 
comprehensive listening test. Such a test might ask the listener to discriminate between 
samples of audio that have either been treated with hybrid artificial reverberation using 
convolution with a real RIR or using convolution with a real RIR up to tmp, followed by 
an algorithmically generated reverberation tail.  
 As this investigation used results from only one receiver position within each room, a 
further investigation that looked at the FD profile of RIRs from various receiver positions 
in the same room could be looked at. It may also be of value to investigate whether a 
large deviation in the FD of the impulse response between ears could be a factor affecting 
tmp. 
 To better understand the factors that influence tmp, in relation to the FD of an RIR, an 
understanding of the psychoacoustic response to the complexity is suggested.

% hfd - Salai Selvam V, 2011 - vsalaiselvam@yahoo.com 
% Higuchi algorithm for calculating Fractal Dimension 
  
function [varargout]=hfd(x,kmax) 
% INPUT: x - time series for HFD is to be estimated; kmax - maximum 
value of k 
% OUTPUT: xhfd - HFD of x; lnk - ln(1/k); lnLk - ln(L(k)) log of average 
curve length, L; Lmk - all values of curve length, L 
  
if ~exist('kmax','var')||isempty(kmax), 
    kmax=5; 
end; 
  
N=length(x); 
  
Lmk=zeros(kmax,kmax); 
for k=1:kmax, 
    for m=1:k, 
        Lmki=0; 
        for i=1:fix((N-m)/k), 
            Lmki=Lmki+abs(x(m+i*k)-x(m+(i-1)*k)); 
        end; 
        Ng=(N-1)/(fix((N-m)/k)*k); 
        Lmk(m,k)=(Lmki*Ng)/k;%Here is the problem in the code by Mr. 
Tikkuhirvi & Mr. Aino 
    end; 
end;

%See "Image method for efficiently simulating small-room acoustics" 
%Allen and Berkley, J.Acoust.Soc.Am April '79 
%This is a matlab version of their fortran code 
  
function delp=lthimage(dr,dr0,rl,nr)     
     
    r2l=zeros(1,3);delp=zeros(1,8);rp=zeros(3,8); 
    %don't need other dimension statements 
    %Loop over all sign permutations and compute r+/-r0 
    i0=1; 
    for l=-1:2:1 
        for j=-1:2:1 
            for k=-1:2:1 
                % nearest image is l=j=k=-1 
                rp(1,i0)=dr(1)+l*dr0(1); 
                rp(2,i0)=dr(2)+j*dr0(2); 
                rp(3,i0)=dr(3)+k*dr0(3); 
                i0=i0+1; 
            end %for k 
        end %for j 
    end %for l      %equivalent of label '10' 
     
% add in mean radius to 8 vectors to get total delay 
    r2l(1)=2*rl(1)*nr(1); 
    r2l(2)=2*rl(2)*nr(2); 
    r2l(3)=2*rl(3)*nr(3); 
  

===== PAGE 68 =====
 
 
 
57 
    for i=1:8 
        delsq=0; 
        for j=1:3 
            r1=r2l(j)-rp(j,i); 
            delsq=delsq+r1.^2; 
        end %for j=1:3 %label is 25 
        delp(i)=round(sqrt(delsq)); %want integer value for delp(i) 
    end %for i %label is '20' 
  
return

9.2.3  Block Convolution 
 
Block convolution takes advantage of the real-time applicability of time domain 
convolution and the efficiency of frequency domain convolution. Three methods will be 
discussed in the following sub-sections: Overlap and Add, Overlap and Save, and Zero-
Delay Convolution.   
 
9.2.3.1 Overlap and Add  
 
 For this method the input signal is broken into smaller sections (or blocks) and 
convolved with an RIR in the frequency domain. The blocks are divided so they are non-
overlapping and sequential. This allows for many shorter convolutions to take place over 
time. Convolution proceeds with the periodicity of the input blocks being exploited so 
that they can be processed through a special form of convolution known as circular 
convolution. 
 Circular convolution can be used when a function gT is periodic, with period T. Then, for 
a function f, such that f * gT exists, whose convolution is also periodic and identical, the 
circular convolution of these functions is defined as

Although this form of block convolution exploits the highly efficient use of frequency 
domain convolution it still requires that the whole first block is loaded before convolution 
can begin, thus resulting in a small delay which may be tolerable for some applications.

9.2.3.3 Zero-Delay Method 
 
Gardiner has proposed a hybrid method of convolution that uses elements of FIR and 
block convolution that produces minimal delay in the output signal (Figure 9.2.3.3a)

This initial suggestion for a method of convolution by Gardiner works by decomposing 
the impulse response into successively larger sections (of the form 2xN) and for direct 
FIR convolution with the input signal. The first output, yo, is calculated by direct FIR 
convolution. During this calculation there is enough time to collect N samples for a block 
convolution of the first N samples to produce y1. In the time for this calculation there is 
enough time to collect 2N samples for the next block convolution. This process continues 
for the whole input length. Due to the inability to increase the block size without violating 
the zero-delay constraint, Gardner rejected this method in preference to another algorithm 
that allows uniform processing.  
This uniform processing is achieved by starting block convolution, with blocks of size N 
that start at least 2N samples into the filter response (Figure 9.2.3.3b). 
 Despite an amount of process scheduling, Gardner’s revised algorithm results in zero-
delay convolution.

9.4.1.5 Pole-Zero Plot 
 
A pole-zero plot is a graphical representation of the transfer function, H(z), in the z-plane. 
This analysis technique helps to reveal information regarding the stability and phase 
extremes of the system. As H(z) is a ratio of polynomials of z, and since polynomials can 
be represented as their roots plus a scale factor, H(z) can be characterised by its 
numerator roots (known as the zeros of the filter), its denominator roots (known as the 
poles of the filter), and a constant gain factor.

9.5.3 Feedback Delay Networks 
 
Feedback delay networks (FDN) were first suggested for use with ADR algorithms by 
Michael Gerzon after observing that individual feedback comb filters yielded poor quality 
sound whereas when several were cross-coupled the result was of superior quality.   
[Gerzon, 1971; Gerzon, 1972]. An FDN is a feedback comb filter with N feedback 
channels where the delay line and feedback gain elements are replaced with matrices. The 
delay line lengths are kept mutually prime to maximise the pseudo-randomness of the 
reverberators.
